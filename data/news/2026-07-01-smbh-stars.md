At the center of our Galaxy, a few stars orbit the supermassive black hole Sagittarius A* on very tight paths. If dark matter piles up around the black hole, these stars plunge through it on every orbit.

With Andrew Gustafson, Nicole Bell, Jayden Newstead, Sandra Robles and Ian Shoemaker, we compute how much energy dark matter would exchange with such a star, using S4714 as an example. It has one of the closest and fastest orbits known. For a dense dark matter spike, the energy deposited can match the star's own luminosity, for interaction strengths that popular dark matter production scenarios predict. This happens without the dark matter having to annihilate, which opens a new route toward so-called dark stars.

These stars are already monitored closely. That makes the Galactic Center a natural laboratory for dark matter interactions, complementary to detectors on Earth.
